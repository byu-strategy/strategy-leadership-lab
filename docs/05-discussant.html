Being a Discussant

Author

Scott Murff

Being a Discussant

Once during the semester you and one or two others own the front of the room for fifteen minutes.

The reading, the quote, and the scripture are assigned. You do not choose them. What you bring is the leader profile, one question of your own, and the ability to get twenty-six people talking.

Everyone takes exactly one turn. Twenty-seven leaders, twelve sessions, two or three discussants a week.

The Job in One Sentence

Get twenty-six busy people to think out loud about one idea for ten minutes.

That is harder than presenting and it is a different skill. Presenting is something you can do alone in a room. Running a discussion means the other people have to talk, which means your preparation is mostly about them, not about you.

What You Actually Do

Week one: sign up. Pick a session. The reading for each session is already assigned, so you are choosing a date and a topic together.

Two weeks out: meet your co-discussants. Fifteen minutes on a call is enough. Decide who takes the quote and scripture, who takes the leader profile, and who opens the discussion. Three of your questions are already written for you. Agree on the fourth.

One week out: read it properly. Not a skim. You need to know what the reading argues, what it gets wrong, and what it would mean for someone running a club at BYU. Bring an opinion.

Tuesday, 11:59 PM: circulate the brief. One page, to the whole class. See below.

Friday, 12:35: run it.

The One-Page Brief

One page. Not two. Sent to the class by Tuesday 11:59 PM so people have three days.

Five parts:

  1. The quote and the scripture, printed, and what you think they share
  2. The reading, with a link and an honest time estimate
  3. What it argues, in three or four sentences
  4. Why it matters here. One paragraph connecting it to something a club leader in this program actually faces
  5. Your own question, the fourth one. Anything you are curious about. Printed, so people can think about it before Friday

The brief exists so that someone who did not finish the reading can still take part. That is not a loophole, it is the design. Twenty-seven club leaders in October will not all finish every reading, and a discussion that only works if everyone did is a discussion that fails half the time.

The Fifteen Minutes

Minutes What
3 Quote and scripture
2 Leader profile
10 Discussion

The quote and the scripture. Assigned, always as a pair, both speaking to the week’s principle from two directions.

Your job is not to find them, it is to land them. Read both out loud, say in one sentence what you think they share, and stop. Do not explain either for two minutes.

Sometimes the pair will not say the identical thing. A quote about decisiveness beside a scripture about patience is not a mistake; it is the mean, put in the room before anyone has argued about it. If you notice the tension, say so. That is usually the best sentence anyone says in the first three minutes.

The leader profile. This one is yours to choose. Three or four sentences: who they are, the decision or moment worth knowing, what it cost them. A real person, living or dead, ideally one nobody in the room would have picked. Pick someone who fits the week’s principle, including someone who got it wrong.

The discussion. Open with a question, not a summary. Everyone read the brief; they do not need you to recap it.

The Three Standing Questions

Every discussion in this course asks the same three questions. They do not change week to week, and you do not have to invent them.

  1. What about this leader or this reading inspires you? Name the principle. Not “they were great,” but which specific quality, and what it looked like in practice. The Principles page has fourteen to choose from.

  2. Where does that principle become a vice? Push it too far and every strength turns into something else. Name what it becomes, and if you can, name a time you have seen the excess in this program.

  3. Under what conditions does the quote stop applying? When would you have acted differently than this leader did, and why?

Then bring one question of your own. Anything you are curious about: something in the reading, something it made you think about, something happening in the program right now. Three questions are standardized so you are not starting from a blank page. The fourth is yours.

You will not get through all four in ten minutes. That is expected. Two good ones beat four rushed ones.

Why These Three Questions

They come from the golden mean, the framework the Principles page lays out.

Aristotle argued that a virtue is not a feeling or a single act but a settled disposition to choose well, and that it sits in a mean between two failure modes: one of doing too little and one of doing too much. Courage is not the opposite of cowardice; it sits between cowardice and recklessness. There are two ways to miss, not one.

That is why leadership advice so often contradicts itself. “Be decisive” and “gather input” are not opposing camps, they are the two ways of missing the same mean. A co-president who never decides is halting. One who decides before anyone has spoken is impulsive. The question is never which advice is right, it is where the mean sits for this decision, this week, with these people.

Most leadership quotes are a maximum dressed up as a truth. Question 2 is how you find the edge, and question 3 is how you find the conditions where the edge matters.

Use the principles table. Naming the principle is the whole first move, and “what is the excess version of this” is much easier to answer once it has a name. All fourteen are on the Principles page with both failure modes already written.

How to Run a Discussion

Six things that decide whether this works:

Open with your hardest question, not your easiest. A warm-up question gets warm-up answers and you have ten minutes.

Make the fourth one arguable. The three standing questions already are. “What did you think of the reading?” produces silence. “The author says you should never escalate to your advisor without telling your co-president first. When is that wrong?” produces an argument. Ask what you actually want to know, not what sounds like a discussion question.

Count to ten in your head. The silence after a question feels much longer to you than to anyone else. Almost every discussion that dies is killed by the facilitator answering their own question at second four.

Call on people by name. You know all twenty-six of them. Use it. “You ran the case workshop last week, does this match what happened?” beats asking the room in general.

Do not answer your own questions. Your opinion is the least useful one in the room during your fifteen minutes.

Follow the fire. If the room catches on question one, ride it and drop the rest. Getting through all four is not the goal.

Grading

Fifteen points, 15% of the course grade.

Points
Brief circulated on time, one page, five parts present 5
Your fourth question is a real question, one you actually want answered 5
You ran a discussion rather than gave a presentation 5

The third one is the whole point. If you and your co-discussants did most of the talking, that is the section that loses points, no matter how well prepared you were.

If You Cannot Make Your Week

Swap with someone. Arrange it in advance and tell Scott. Do not spend your free absence on the one session where twenty-six people are counting on you.
